How We Detect Meteors

How automated cameras record meteors above Billingborough and contribute observations to a much larger picture of meteor activity across the world.

STARTING WITH THE BASICS

What is a meteor?

A meteor is the visible streak of light produced when a small piece of material from space enters Earth's atmosphere at high speed.

The object travelling through space is called a meteoroid. The brief flash of light we see in the atmosphere is the meteor. If any surviving material reaches the ground, it is called a meteorite.

Most meteors are fleeting events. They may last only a fraction of a second, making them easy to miss if nobody happens to be looking at exactly the right moment.

The sky doesn't wait for us.

An automated camera can keep watching throughout the night, recording events that would otherwise go unnoticed.

HOW IT WORKS

From a flash of light to an observation

The cameras operate automatically, recording the night sky frame after frame. When a meteor crosses the field of view, it can appear as a short bright streak against the background stars.

The resulting images can then be examined and processed to determine whether a meteor has been recorded and to extract useful information about the event.

This is one of the important advantages of automated observation: the camera can be collecting data while nobody is physically present at the observatory.

A MUCH BIGGER PICTURE

From Billingborough to the Global Meteor Network

A meteor observed from one camera provides useful information, but observations become much more powerful when they can be compared with observations from other locations.

The Global Meteor Network brings together observations from meteor cameras around the world. By combining data from different observing locations, researchers can study meteor trajectories and the distribution and activity of meteoroids entering Earth's atmosphere.

This means that a meteor recorded above Billingborough is not simply an interesting local event. It can become one observation within a much larger scientific dataset.

Local observation. Global science.

Our cameras observe the sky from Lincolnshire, while the data they produce contributes to an international network of meteor observers.

WHAT CAN WE LEARN?

Why record meteors?

Regular meteor observations can help astronomers investigate how material is distributed through the Solar System and how it enters Earth's atmosphere.

Repeated observations also allow meteor showers and individual meteor events to be studied over time. The more observations that can be collected, the better the overall picture becomes.

For an amateur observatory, this provides an opportunity to take part in genuine astronomical observation rather than simply observing objects for visual enjoyment.
