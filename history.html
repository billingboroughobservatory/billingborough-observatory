ASTRONOMICAL HERITAGE

The Billingborough Astronomer

Edward Iszatt Essam and the observatory that stood in Billingborough more than a century ago.

Billingborough Post Office and drug store, formerly operated by Edward Iszatt Essam. Photograph reproduced with permission of the Aveland Archive and Aveland History Group.

A village with an astronomical past

Billingborough has a remarkable astronomical history. At the centre of that story was Edward Iszatt Essam (1855–1931), a chemist, druggist, postmaster, photographer and active amateur astronomer who lived and worked in the village during the closing years of the nineteenth century.

Essam operated the village Post Office and drug store from 1893 until 1907. During these years he also established a private observatory and became an active contributor to the observing programmes of the British Astronomical Association.

His surviving astronomical records reveal an observer who studied Jupiter and Saturn, recorded stellar colours, observed solar eclipses and reported transient phenomena including an aurora.

Astronomy from Billingborough

Essam's observations were not simply private notes. They were submitted to the British Astronomical Association and became part of the organised astronomical observing programme of the period.

Edward Iszatt Essam

Edward Iszatt Essam was born in Lincolnshire in 1855 at Aslackby Fen, a short distance from Billingborough. He trained as a chemist and eventually established himself in the village as a chemist and druggist.

In 1893 Essam succeeded William Forster Smith as proprietor of the village Post Office and drug store. He remained in Billingborough until 1907, when his business came to an end and he left the village.

The surviving historical record also describes Essam as a photographer and telescope maker. His professional life therefore existed alongside a wide range of scientific and technical interests.

Essam and the British Astronomical Association

Essam was involved with the British Astronomical Association from its early years. A BAA membership record records Edward Essam of Lincolnshire among members elected in 1892.

British Astronomical Association membership record, 1892, recording Edward Essam of Lincolnshire among newly elected members. Source: British Astronomical Association. Reproduced with permission.

During the 1890s he contributed observations to the Association's Jupiter and Saturn sections. His work included detailed observations and drawings of Saturn and its rings.

His astronomical work was subsequently recognised more widely. Essam was elected a Fellow of the Royal Astronomical Society in 1898.

Essam's Observatory

During his years in Billingborough, Essam established a private astronomical observatory associated with his home.

Edward Essam and Dr Thomas Blassom with Blassom's telescope, c.1895. Photograph courtesy of Robin Freer. Original photograph held by Lincs Inspire, Grimsby Local History Library, ref. NEL13979.

The surviving historical survey records a substantial collection of instruments associated with the observatory, including an 11-inch reflector, a 12-inch reflector and a 3-inch refractor.

British Astronomical Association equipment record listing Essam of Billingborough with a 12-inch reflector, a 3-inch refractor and a 4¼-inch optical instrument. Source: British Astronomical Association. Reproduced with permission.

This contemporary BAA record provides particularly valuable evidence of the instruments available to Essam. It lists a 12-inch reflector, a 3-inch refractor and a 4¼-inch optical instrument.

The precise form and location of the observatory remain subjects of historical investigation. Although its existence and astronomical activity are well supported, we do not yet have sufficient evidence to reconstruct the physical building with confidence.

The observatory remains a mystery

One of the aims of the continuing research into Essam is to establish the precise location of his observatory and discover whether contemporary maps, directories, photographs or property records can reveal more about its construction.

Observing the solar eclipse of 1895

One of the most revealing surviving records is Essam's report of the partial solar eclipse of 26 March 1895.

Observing from Billingborough, Essam recorded first contact at 9:59 GMT. He carefully described the irregular appearance of the Moon's limb and the changing appearance of lunar mountain peaks projected against the Sun.

He recorded the greatest phase at 10:15 and noted the gradual withdrawal of the Moon's shadow beginning at approximately 10:32.

The report identifies the instrument used as a 4¼-inch refractor operating at a magnification of ×40.

Essam's 1895 eclipse observation

Contemporary British Astronomical Association records preserve Essam's own report of the partial solar eclipse of 26 March 1895, made from Billingborough.

Extract from the British Astronomical Association report of the partial solar eclipse of 26 March 1895, including Essam's observation from Billingborough. Source: British Astronomical Association. Reproduced with permission.

Essam recorded first contact at 9:59 GMT, described irregularities in the Moon's limb and noted apparent lunar mountain peaks projected against the Sun. He recorded the greatest phase at 10:15 and the withdrawal of the Moon's shadow at approximately 10:32.

He identified the instrument used as a 4¼-inch refractor with a power of ×40.

Observing Saturn

Essam was an active member of the BAA Saturn Section. Contemporary reports describe drawings of Saturn that he submitted to the Association.

One report records a carefully executed drawing of Saturn sent by Essam to the Section. His observations included the appearance of Cassini's Division, the prominent gap in Saturn's rings.

Contemporary British Astronomical Association report discussing Essam's observations of Saturn and Cassini's Division. Source: British Astronomical Association. Reproduced with permission.

These observations illustrate the nature of nineteenth-century planetary astronomy. Observers relied upon repeated visual observations, careful drawings and comparison with observations made by astronomers elsewhere.

Jupiter

Essam also contributed observations to the BAA Jupiter Section. His participation placed his Billingborough observatory within a national network of amateur observers whose observations were collected and compared by the Association.

An aurora over Billingborough

Essam's interests extended beyond planned observations of the planets. In correspondence to the BAA he described an auroral display observed from his garden at Billingborough in February 1894.

Extract from Essam's correspondence to the British Astronomical Association describing an auroral display observed from his garden in Billingborough in February 1894. Source: British Astronomical Association. Reproduced with permission.

He described a large streamer rising from the western horizon and changing in appearance and direction as the display developed. His account provides another direct contemporary record of Essam watching and recording the sky from Billingborough.

The colours of the stars

Essam participated in the BAA's Star Colours Section, which collected systematic visual observations of the apparent colours of stars.

British Astronomical Association records concerning Edward Essam and the Star Colours Section. Source: British Astronomical Association. Reproduced with permission.

By the beginning of the twentieth century Essam had taken on a formal role within the section. The BAA's current historical record lists him as Director from 1901 to 1902.

A historical printed BAA record in the material reproduced here gives a different end date for his directorship. The discrepancy is retained rather than silently resolved, and remains part of the ongoing historical investigation.

From Billingborough to Lancashire

Essam's Billingborough years came to an end in 1907. He subsequently moved north, first to Luddenden Foot in Yorkshire and later to Lancashire.

The observatory therefore belonged to a relatively short period in Billingborough's history, but the surviving astronomical records demonstrate that the village was connected to the wider British astronomical community during the late nineteenth century.

A continuing astronomical story

More than a century later, Billingborough Observatory continues that astronomical tradition using modern telescopes, digital cameras and automated meteor detection.

The instruments have changed enormously. The curiosity has not.

Historical research continues

Research into Edward Iszatt Essam's observatory, instruments, observations and life is ongoing. Where the surviving evidence is uncertain, this website distinguishes documented facts from interpretation and questions that remain unresolved.
