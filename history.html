ASTRONOMICAL HERITAGE

The Billingborough Astronomer

Edward Iszatt Essam and the observatory that stood in Billingborough more than a century ago.

Billingborough Post Office and drug store, formerly operated by Edward Iszatt Essam. Photograph reproduced with permission of the Aveland Archive and Aveland History Group.

A village with an astronomical past

Billingborough has a remarkable astronomical history. At the centre of that story was Edward Iszatt Essam (1855–1931), a chemist, druggist, postmaster, photographer and active amateur astronomer who lived and worked in the village during the closing years of the nineteenth century.

Essam operated the village Post Office and drug store from 1893 until 1907. During these years he also established a private observatory and became an active contributor to the observing programmes of the British Astronomical Association.

His surviving astronomical records reveal an observer who studied Jupiter and Saturn, recorded stellar colours, observed solar eclipses and reported transient phenomena including an aurora.

Astronomy from Billingborough

Essam's observations were not simply private notes. They were submitted to the British Astronomical Association and became part of the organised astronomical observing programme of the period.

Edward Iszatt Essam

Edward Iszatt Essam was born in Lincolnshire in 1855 at Aslackby Fen, a short distance from Billingborough. He trained as a chemist and eventually established himself in the village as a chemist and druggist.

In 1893 Essam succeeded William Forster Smith as proprietor of the village Post Office and drug store. He remained in Billingborough until 1907, when his business came to an end and he left the village.

The surviving historical record also describes Essam as a photographer and telescope maker. His professional life therefore existed alongside a wide range of scientific and technical interests.

Essam and the British Astronomical Association

Essam was involved with the British Astronomical Association from its early years. A BAA membership record records Edward Essam of Lincolnshire among members elected in 1892.

British Astronomical Association membership record, 1892, recording Edward Essam of Lincolnshire among newly elected members. Source: British Astronomical Association. Reproduced with permission.

During the 1890s he contributed observations to the Association's Jupiter and Saturn sections. His work included detailed observations and drawings of Saturn and its rings.

His astronomical work was subsequently recognised more widely. Essam was elected a Fellow of the Royal Astronomical Society in 1898.

Essam's Observatory

During his years in Billingborough, Essam established a private astronomical observatory associated with his home. The surviving records suggest that it was not simply a place where commercially produced telescopes were used: Essam also had the practical skills to construct astronomical optical equipment himself.

Indeed, by June 1892 Essam was already publishing observations made with a refracting telescope which he described as being of his own manufacture. This was shortly before he was elected to membership of the British Astronomical Association.

The surviving evidence points to an unusually substantial and evolving collection of instruments. Essam reported using an 8½-inch self-made mirror in 1893, a 4¼-inch refractor of his own production in 1894, an 8½-inch telescope for Jupiter in 1896, and a 12-inch equatorial reflector for Jupiter in 1897.

These reports correspond closely with the instruments recorded in contemporary British Astronomical Association records, although the precise dates on which each instrument was acquired or replaced are not yet established.

Edward Essam and Dr Thomas Blassom with Blassom's telescope, c.1895. Photograph courtesy of Robin Freer. Original photograph held by Lincs Inspire, Grimsby Local History Library, ref. NEL13979.

A contemporary report published in The English Mechanic and World of Science in May 1893 provides particularly revealing evidence of Essam's equipment and practical abilities. Reporting an observation of Saturn made from Billingborough, Essam stated that he was using an 8½-inch mirror with an 88-inch focal length, of his own manufacture, temporarily mounted at the time. He recorded using powers of ×120 and ×240.

The instrument was clearly still in use several years later. In February 1896 Essam reported observing Jupiter with an 8½-inch telescope at a magnification of ×140, again from Billingborough.

Essam's own telescope, 1893

This is important evidence that Essam was not merely an observer but was also constructing astronomical optics himself. The report describes an 8½-inch mirror of 88-inch focal length as being "of my own manufacture".

The observation also demonstrates the capabilities of the instrument. Essam described Saturn's rings, Cassini's Division and several satellites, using magnifications of ×120 and ×240.

Essam's 4¼-inch refractor, 1894

Extract from Edward Essam's report of his observation of Saturn from Billingborough, published in The English Mechanic and World of Science, Vol. 59, 23 February 1894, p. 10.

In another report, Essam described an observation of Saturn made on 23 January 1894. He recorded the appearance of the rings, the shadow of Saturn on the rings, the North Temperate Belt and polar shading.

Particularly significant is his statement that the instrument was a 4¼-inch refractor of his own production, used at powers of ×60 to ×100.

Source: The English Mechanic and World of Science, Vol. 59, 23 February 1894, p. 10.

PRIMARY SOURCE

Essam observes Jupiter, 1896

Extract from Edward Essam's report of his observation of Jupiter from Billingborough, published in The English Mechanic and World of Science, Vol. 63, 28 February 1896, p. 35.

Essam reported an observation of Jupiter made at 8:25 GMT on 9 February 1896, using a power of ×140 on an 8½-inch telescope.

The following year provides particularly strong evidence of Essam's participation in contemporary planetary astronomy. On 9 February 1896 he observed Jupiter from Billingborough using an 8½-inch telescope at a power of ×140, recording changes in the planet's belts and a prominent Great Red Spot.

His drawing was subsequently published in The English Mechanic and World of Science, where it attracted comment from another observer in the following issue.

Source: The English Mechanic and World of Science, Vol. 63, 28 February 1896, p. 35.

CONTEMPORARY CORRESPONDENCE

Essam's Jupiter drawing prompts discussion

Correspondence referring to Edward Essam's Jupiter drawing published in The English Mechanic and World of Science, Vol. 63, 6 March 1896, p. 55.

In the following issue, another observer referred directly to Essam's Jupiter drawing published on 28 February and questioned the appearance of features in Jupiter's equatorial and temperate belts.

The exchange shows that Essam's observations were being read, compared and discussed by other contemporary observers. His drawings therefore formed part of a wider network of visual planetary observation rather than remaining solely as private records.

Source: The English Mechanic and World of Science, Vol. 63, 6 March 1896, p. 55.

PRIMARY SOURCE

Essam's 12-inch reflector, 1897

Edward Essam's report of an observation of Jupiter made on 21 April 1897 from Billingborough, published in The English Mechanic and World of Science.

In 1897 Essam contributed another Jupiter observation to The English Mechanic and World of Science, following a detailed discussion of observations of the planet by other contributors.

He stated that the observation had been made on 21 April 1897 using a 12-inch equatorial reflector, at powers of ×176 and ×440. The observation was accompanied by a sketch of Jupiter.

This provides independent contemporary evidence for the substantial 12-inch reflector later listed in British Astronomical Association records as belonging to Essam at Billingborough.

Source: The English Mechanic and World of Science, 1897.

Other contemporary BAA records show that Essam had access to several different instruments. A BAA equipment record lists him at Billingborough with a 12-inch reflector, a 3-inch refractor and a 4¼-inch optical instrument.

British Astronomical Association equipment record listing Essam of Billingborough with a 12-inch reflector, a 3-inch refractor and a 4¼-inch optical instrument. Source: British Astronomical Association. Reproduced with permission.

His 1895 report of the partial solar eclipse provides further evidence of the variety of instruments available to him: on that occasion he specifically identified the instrument used as a 4¼-inch refractor operating at ×40.

Taken together, these records suggest that Essam's astronomical equipment changed over time or that he maintained more than one instrument. The precise relationship between the 8½-inch self-made mirror described in 1893 and the larger instruments recorded by the BAA later in the decade remains uncertain.

The observatory remains a mystery

One of the aims of the continuing research into Essam is to establish the precise location of his observatory and discover whether contemporary maps, directories, photographs or property records can reveal more about its construction.

Observing the solar eclipse of 1895

One of the most revealing surviving records is Essam's report of the partial solar eclipse of 26 March 1895.

Observing from Billingborough, Essam recorded first contact at 9:59 GMT. He carefully described the irregular appearance of the Moon's limb and the changing appearance of lunar mountain peaks projected against the Sun.

He recorded the greatest phase at 10:15 and noted the gradual withdrawal of the Moon's shadow beginning at approximately 10:32.

The report identifies the instrument used as a 4¼-inch refractor operating at a magnification of ×40.

Observing Saturn

Saturn was one of Essam's principal astronomical interests. Contemporary records show him making detailed visual observations of the planet from Billingborough and submitting observations and drawings to astronomical publications and societies.

In May 1893, for example, Essam reported an observation of Saturn made from Billingborough in unusually revealing detail.

Edward Iszatt Essam's report of his observation of Saturn, published in The English Mechanic and World of Science, Vol. 57, 26 May 1893, p. 310.

Essam reported very good definition and described the shadow of Saturn's rings upon the planet, Cassini's Division, the North Temperate Belt and a fainter feature near the North Polar Region. He also recorded four satellites, probably Titan, Rhea, Dione and Iapetus.

Particularly significantly, Essam stated that he was using an 8½-inch mirror with an 88-inch focal length, of his own manufacture, temporarily mounted at the time. He recorded using powers of ×120 and ×240.

Source: The English Mechanic and World of Science, Vol. 57, 26 May 1893, p. 310.

Essam was also an active member of the BAA Saturn Section. Contemporary reports describe drawings of Saturn that he submitted to the Association.

Contemporary British Astronomical Association report discussing Essam's observations of Saturn and Cassini's Division. Source: British Astronomical Association. Reproduced with permission.

These observations illustrate the nature of nineteenth-century planetary astronomy. Observers relied upon repeated visual observations, careful drawings and comparison with observations made by astronomers elsewhere.

Jupiter

Essam also contributed observations to the BAA Jupiter Section. His participation placed his Billingborough observatory within a national network of amateur observers whose observations were collected and compared by the Association.

An aurora over Billingborough

Essam's interests extended beyond planned observations of the planets. In correspondence to the BAA he described an auroral display observed from his garden at Billingborough in February 1894.

Extract from Essam's correspondence to the British Astronomical Association describing an auroral display observed from his garden in Billingborough in February 1894. Source: British Astronomical Association. Reproduced with permission.

He described a large streamer rising from the western horizon and changing in appearance and direction as the display developed. His account provides another direct contemporary record of Essam watching and recording the sky from Billingborough.

The colours of the stars

Essam participated in the BAA's Star Colours Section, which collected systematic visual observations of the apparent colours of stars.

British Astronomical Association records concerning Edward Essam and the Star Colours Section. Source: British Astronomical Association. Reproduced with permission.

By the beginning of the twentieth century Essam had taken on a formal role within the section. The BAA's current historical record lists him as Director from 1901 to 1902.

A historical printed BAA record in the material reproduced here gives a different end date for his directorship. The discrepancy is retained rather than silently resolved, and remains part of the ongoing historical investigation.

From Billingborough to Lancashire

Essam's Billingborough years came to an end in 1907. He subsequently moved north, first to Luddenden Foot in Yorkshire and later to Lancashire.

The observatory therefore belonged to a relatively short period in Billingborough's history, but the surviving astronomical records demonstrate that the village was connected to the wider British astronomical community during the late nineteenth century.

A continuing astronomical story

More than a century later, Billingborough Observatory continues that astronomical tradition using modern telescopes, digital cameras and automated meteor detection.

The instruments have changed enormously. The curiosity has not.

Historical research continues

Research into Edward Iszatt Essam's observatory, instruments, observations and life is ongoing. Where the surviving evidence is uncertain, this website distinguishes documented facts from interpretation and questions that remain unresolved.
